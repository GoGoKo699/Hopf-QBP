# VIII Conclusion

The Hopf tree carries an objective response backward as well as a state forward. Under phase-calibrated controlled-reflection access, the global construction maps the reflected response to computational addresses for all normalized magnitude directions. One shared record stream recovers the complete magnitude block, and a separate signed leaf record supplies the complex phase derivatives. The magnitude stream alone completes the real chart; the two streams together complete the complex chart. Checkpointing provides the layer-local alternative, reversing only the suffix below a chosen depth to recover that layer’s derivative block.

The reverse construction uses the same direct-angle geometry as the Hopf representation. Each magnitude coordinate defines one nested amplitude split, is programmed as the physical angle of that split, and determines one differential direction. Each complex leaf phase is likewise a directly programmed control. The same recursion prepares the state; its classical inverse recovers coordinates from known amplitudes, and its diagonal metric supplies the corresponding weights. Representation, circuit control, and differentiation therefore remain in one language. The circuit structure that defines the model also supplies the directions in which its objective response is decoded.

At fixed additive coordinate accuracy and confidence, sufficient execution counts are $`\mathcal{O}(\log\log M)`$ for the global method and $`\mathcal{O}(\log M\log\log M)`$ for the complete checkpoint schedule. The global gain is record reuse across magnitude coordinates, not a smaller classical output. Under the designated direct-angle ledger, the global construction meets the logarithmic matched-resource benchmark relative to a scalar evaluation using the same forward preparation and controlled reflection. Checkpointing is complementary for a selected layer with a less costly reverse suffix. These guarantees concern raw coordinate derivatives: the $`M`$-entry output, classical decoding, and conditioning of relative or metric-rescaled accuracy remain separate costs and requirements.

Applications require the stated coherent objective interface or a suitable reflection-sum decomposition. Within that condition, complete coordinate updates may support variational scientific computing, pure-state learning, and probe or decision-state design, while checkpoint records may support layer-local quantum-control updates. These are uses of the gradient interface, not application-level performance guarantees. The data encoding, controlled-reflection compiler, aggregation rule, and optimizer must still be specified for each problem.

The principal open question is whether the coherent objective interface can be weakened without losing the shared reverse record. Alternative interference or measurement constructions may expose the same transition moments with different control requirements. A second direction is to adapt the Hopf organization to structured state families whose relevant degrees of freedom occupy only part of the full pure-state sphere. The two papers thus make the direct-angle Hopf geometry bidirectional: the compass maps coordinates to any pure state, and quantum backpropagation through the controlled-reflection mirror returns the objective response to them.
